A building team is reviewing a temperature-monitoring outage. Sensors in twelve rooms send readings every five minutes to a collector. The collector writes readings to a local queue and forwards them to a dashboard. An alert fires if a room has no visible reading for fifteen minutes. The system does not control heating or ventilation; staff use it to notice problems. During a Tuesday afternoon outage, eight rooms showed no new readings, while four continued to update normally.

At 13:42 a technician changed the Wi-Fi access point in the east corridor. At 13:47 the collector logged repeated connection failures for sensor IDs E1 through E8. At 13:50 the dashboard received its last reading from those sensors. At 14:05 the first missing-reading alert was delivered. The technician reverted the access-point configuration at 14:14. Readings resumed at 14:18. Four west-corridor sensors use a different access point and had no failures. The technician had changed the network name but kept the password, assuming clients would reconnect automatically.

The collector's local disk filled at 14:11. A cleanup task removed older queue files at 14:16, and disk usage fell from 100 percent to 63 percent. This is a second event, but the queue had already stopped receiving east-corridor readings before the disk filled. Forwarding logs show successful dashboard deliveries for west-corridor readings throughout the incident, apart from a two-minute pause at 14:11. There is no retained packet capture. The team cannot prove whether every east sensor retried at the same rate or whether some attempts were lost before reaching the collector.

One draft incident report says 'disk exhaustion caused the whole outage'. Another says 'the access-point change caused all missing data'. Neither statement fully reflects the timeline. East sensors buffer only one reading locally, so readings during a prolonged disconnection can be overwritten. The collector's queue preserves readings it has already received, but it cannot recover measurements that never arrived. After reconnection, the dashboard showed new readings and one older reading per east sensor. That behavior is consistent with the documented sensor buffer, though it does not establish exactly how many measurements were lost.

Staff want three actions before the next maintenance window. First, the access-point checklist should include checking connected sensor IDs before and after a change. Second, queue disk usage needs an alert before reaching capacity. Third, someone should test how sensor buffering behaves during a controlled short disconnection. A proposal to buy an entirely new sensor system has no cost estimate, and the team has not established that replacement is necessary. The report should be candid about evidence limits while giving useful next steps.

Write a short incident explanation for nontechnical building staff. Separate the likely trigger, the additional disk problem, and the uncertainty about lost measurements. Give the three practical actions and avoid assigning personal blame.
