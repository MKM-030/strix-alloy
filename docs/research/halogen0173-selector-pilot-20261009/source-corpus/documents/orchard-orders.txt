An orchard cooperative needs to choose how many reusable crates to reserve for a weekend sale. Crates hold 12 kilograms each. The cooperative expects to harvest 1,140 kilograms of apples on Friday and 780 kilograms on Saturday. Ten percent of each day's harvest is set aside for damaged fruit and processing. The remaining saleable apples are packed into crates. A partially filled crate still occupies one crate. Packing is done separately each day because Friday's shipment leaves before Saturday's harvest begins.

Three retailers have placed orders. North Shop wants 52 crates on Friday. East Shop wants 31 crates on Friday and 26 on Saturday. South Shop wants 28 crates on Saturday. The cooperative also runs a stall. It wants to offer at least 16 crates on each day, but unsold stall fruit may be returned to storage. Retailer orders have priority over the stall, and no retailer agrees to receive less than their order. Apples from Friday that remain after those orders and the Friday stall allocation may be held for Saturday, provided they were packed in complete crates.

The transport company has offered 160 reusable crates for the entire weekend. It does not collect empty crates until Monday, so a crate that leaves for a retailer cannot be reused on Saturday. Friday stall crates can be reused on Saturday after the stall closes. If a crate is only partly emptied at the stall, the remaining fruit can be transferred to storage boxes, freeing that crate. Storage boxes are already available and do not need to be reserved. There is no limit on the number of boxes.

The cooperative's draft calculation says that 1,920 kilograms requires 160 crates, so the offer is exactly sufficient. A grower points out that this ignores damaged fruit. Another person says the damage allowance means only 144 crates are needed. That second figure ignores daily rounding and retailer allocations. The coordinator wants a careful calculation that follows the order of events. The cooperative cannot count a crate returned by a retailer before Monday, and it cannot sell processing fruit at the stall to cover a shortfall.

There is an additional delivery option. East Shop can move up to six of its Friday crates to Saturday, with no change to its total order. North Shop cannot move any crates, and South Shop cannot collect earlier. If the planned stall minimum is impossible, the coordinator wants to know whether this option fixes the problem. A moved crate becomes part of Saturday's retailer requirement. It is not a new order. The transport company's crate limit applies to the largest number of physical crates simultaneously tied up, not to the sum of all packing actions over the weekend.

Determine whether all original retailer orders and both stall minimums can be met with the available fruit and crates. If moving some East Shop delivery helps, say how many must move and explain why. Show the key quantities and distinguish fruit supply from reusable-crate supply.
